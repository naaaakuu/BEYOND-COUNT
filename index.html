CARBON DATING: COUNTING THE UNRECORDED PAST

When an ancient wooden pillar from Ashoka's capital or a Harappan relic is excavated from the earth, centuries have blurred beyond all historical recollection. How does science count time when no calendar survived? By counting the silent, ticking atomic clock of Carbon-14.

Fig 1: The Cosmic & Biological Cycle of Carbon-14 (Radiocarbon Chronology) Half-Life: 5,730 ± 40 Years
STEP 01

Cosmic Ray Creation

High-energy cosmic neutrons strike atmospheric Nitrogen-14 (14N) in the upper sky, transforming it into radioactive Carbon-14 (14C). This oxidizes into 14CO2.

STEP 02

Living Assimilation

Plants absorb 14CO2 during photosynthesis; animals consume plants. Throughout an organism's lifetime, its ratio of 14C to stable 12C remains identical to the atmosphere.

STEP 03

Clock Starts at Death

The moment a tree falls or an animal dies, carbon intake ceases. Stable Carbon-12 stays unchanged, but Carbon-14 decays exponentially through beta decay back into Nitrogen-14 with a half-life of 5,730 years.

STEP 04

Archaeological Dating

By measuring the surviving fraction of Carbon-14 using Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS), archaeologists calculate exactly how many thousands of years have elapsed since the organic material died.

The Radioactive Decay Law

N(t) = N₀ × (½)(t / 5730)

Where N(t) is remaining Carbon-14, N₀ is the original equilibrium level, and t is elapsed time. Every 5,730 years, exactly 50% of remaining atoms decay.

When Willard Libby invented radiocarbon dating in 1949 (awarded the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), it revolutionized archaeology by establishing absolute dates for prehistoric human cultures across the Indian subcontinent.

How Ancient India Conceived Numbers & Infinity

1. Sāṅkhyeya (Countable)

In the Jain Mathematical Canon (c. 500 BCE) and the Sulba Sutras, numbers were classified starting with finite, distinct quantities that can be counted step by step.

2. Asaṅkhyeya (Uncountable)

Quantities that have definite bounds but are beyond human observational counting (like the grains of cotton fiber or leaves in a forest). Ancient Indian scholars created massive named powers of ten up to 10140 (such as Asankhyeya and Tallakshana) to express them.

3. Ananta (The Infinite)

Mathematicians like Brahmagupta (628 CE) and Bhāskara II (1150 CE) recognized Khahara—a number divided by zero becomes infinity. The Isha Upanishad declared: subtracting infinity from infinity still leaves infinity.
